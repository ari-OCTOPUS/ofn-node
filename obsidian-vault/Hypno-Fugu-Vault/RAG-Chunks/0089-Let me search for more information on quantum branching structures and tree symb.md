Trees Are Physical Objects with Information-Processing Architecture
Trees grow through cambial activity that produces branching structures following allometric scaling laws between length, thickness, and dry mass. Wind loads and competition for light sculpt trees into self-similar structures through evolutionary optimization. The branching structure can be modeled as a hierarchical graph where each branch is characterized by position, orientation, length, and offspring count.

Trees also process information electrically. Plants transmit action potentials, calcium waves, and reactive oxygen species (ROS) waves through vascular networks for rapid systemic signaling. A 60-channel multielectrode array (MEA) study of root apices revealed spatiotemporal electrical network activity with patterns reminiscent of neural networks. Plants can communicate with neighboring plants through mycorrhizal networks ("wood wide web"), enabling bidirectional carbon transfer between species.

Tree Rings Are Temporal Encodings
Dendrochronology demonstrates that trees literally encode time into spatial structure. Seasonal cambial growth creates ring patterns that record climate signals, enabling cross-dated chronologies spanning thousands of years. This is a direct biological instance of temporal information being spatially encoded—a pattern with deep analogues in physics (spacetime) and neuroscience (memory traces).

River Networks Follow Optimal Branching
Optimal Channel Networks (OCNs) model river drainage as tree structures that minimize total gravitational energy loss. These models reproduce real river network scaling laws, including universal tributary length and spacing relationships. River networks exhibit scale-invariant branching with power-law distributions for node in-degree and component sizes.
