# Learning science — Research Digest 2026-07-05 (live kickoff)

## Today's question
Interleaved practice (mixing topics/skills, ABCABC) is sold as a "desirable difficulty" that beats blocked practice (AAABBBCCC) for durable learning — but is that true across the board, and **under what conditions does interleaving actually fail or backfire**? This matters because Ari's whole vault is built on spaced/interleaved agent loops and self-directed skill practice; knowing the boundary conditions stops us from over-applying a rule of thumb.

## Findings

- **[peer-reviewed] The interleaving benefit is real but moderate and highly material-dependent — not universal.** The largest meta-analysis (59 studies, 238 effect sizes) found an overall interleaving advantage of only *g ≈ 0.42*, and it swung hard by material type: strong for visual/inductive categories (paintings *g ≈ 0.67*), small for math (*g ≈ 0.34*), ambiguous/non-significant for expository texts, and *reversed* for memorizing individual words (*g ≈ −0.39*, i.e. blocking won). The rule of thumb: interleaving helps most when categories look *similar between* each other and you need to learn to *discriminate* them. **Takeaway for Ari:** don't blanket-interleave. Interleave when the goal is telling confusable things apart (chart patterns, lead-quality categories, spelling/design rules); block when the goal is drilling one discrete item into memory. [source](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000209)

- **[peer-reviewed] Whether blocking or interleaving wins flips depending on the learner's *strategy/goal* — memorize vs. find-the-rule.** A 2025 study (Behavioral Sciences/MDPI) replicated and extended a key interaction over a ~48h delay: when the task is *memory-based* (classify by similarity), interleaving wins; when the task is *rule-based* (apply an articulable rule), **blocking wins**. Same materials — only the instruction changed the optimal schedule, and the pattern held after a delay. **Takeaway:** the "best" practice schedule isn't a property of the content, it's a property of *what kind of knowledge you're trying to build*. For agent-design and for Ari's own study, first ask "am I memorizing exemplars or inducing a rule?" before choosing a schedule. [source](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-328X/15/5/662)

- **[peer-reviewed] For novices / low-prior-knowledge learners, pure interleaving is an *undesirable* difficulty — hybrid (block-then-interleave) beats both.** In 107 low-achieving adolescents learning L2 vocabulary, interleaving alone hurt; early *blocked* practice was needed to first build declarative knowledge, and a **hybrid A/A/A→A/B/C schedule produced the most robust long-term retention** — outperforming both pure blocking and pure interleaving. Spelling-rule work in children (N=147, 8-week follow-up) echoes it: interleaving's transfer benefit accrued **mainly to high-prior-knowledge learners**. **Takeaway:** desirable difficulty has a floor — you have to be *able* to do the task for the struggle to pay off. Sequence Ari's (and agents') learning as block-to-build, then interleave-to-discriminate; don't throw a beginner straight into mixed practice. [source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4a81a0c1051c59a9a7114aa43cbcb95e/) · [spelling study](https://consensus.app/papers/details/e3f870f9e3c1572f9655ca0ef21d8173/)

- **[peer-reviewed] "Interleaving" and "spacing" are *different mechanisms* and shouldn't be conflated.** A systematic review argues the **spacing** effect works by giving working memory *rest-from-deliberate-learning* (a cognitive-load recovery effect), while **interleaving** works by the *discriminative-contrast* mechanism (juxtaposition helps you see category boundaries). Interleaving delivers spacing *without* the rest — so its benefit can't be explained by spacing alone, and inserting a distractor task that disrupts the side-by-side contrast can *eliminate* the interleaving benefit entirely. **Takeaway:** you get two separable levers. Use **spacing** (rest gaps, revisit-later) for anything memory-heavy and fatiguing; use **interleaving** (tight juxtaposition, no interruption between the contrasted items) specifically to sharpen discrimination. Designing them as one knob wastes both. [source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/4d99f512f0e55fb6a869ad3c3a99db11/)

- **[peer-reviewed] The payoff shows up at delayed test, not during practice — and it even reaches *implicit* skill.** Across this literature the signature is consistent: interleaved practice looks *worse during acquisition* (slower, more errors, feels harder) but *better at delayed retention and transfer*. A motor-sequence study showed the benefit extends even to learners with **no explicit awareness** of what they learned, and left them more robust to novel test conditions, whereas blocked-trained learners were "unprepared" when the test format shifted. **Takeaway:** judge a practice method by delayed performance and transfer, never by how smooth it feels in the moment — the felt-fluency of blocking is exactly the trap (and it's the same expectation/fluency-illusion caution the hypnosis scout raised about subjective improvement). [source](https://consensus.app/papers/details/71ee7d04817859f1a01e8e9854f4ac56/)
