## Findings

- **The Iran-US ceasefire and Strait of Hormuz reopening are real but shallow: a 60-day ceasefire and a memorandum of understanding were signed in June 2026, but actual shipping traffic through the strait remains very low, and the UAE's state oil company estimates full flow won't resume until 2027 even in a best-case deal.** Security risk premium in oil (~US$10/bbl) is being priced in on top of already-elevated Brent forecasts (~US$60-62/bbl). → *For Ari's context:* this is a slow, incomplete de-escalation, not a resolved crisis — energy and freight costs should be expected to stay structurally elevated through 2026 rather than snap back to pre-crisis levels. [PBS](https://www.pbs.org/newshour/world/iran-and-u-s-reach-an-initial-deal-to-extend-the-ceasefire-and-open-the-strait-of-hormuz-but-challenges-remain) · [Strait of Hormuz crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2026_Strait_of_Hormuz_crisis)

- **Container freight rates have spiked sharply on the back of Red Sea avoidance plus Hormuz disruption: the Shanghai Containerized Freight Index composite jumped 16% in a single week, Shanghai-to-US spot rates are up 59-129% since late February, and effective global shipping capacity is down roughly 19% (12% from Red Sea avoidance, 2% slow-steaming, 5% port congestion).** Asia-Europe rates sit 25-40% above pre-crisis levels. → *For Ari's context:* this directly raises the landed AUD cost of any imported hardware — Orange Pi 5 Pro / ESP32 restocks for Mining, and any imported goods/supplies for Ziman Galerry — compounding the weak-AUD effect already flagged in yesterday's markets digest. [Lloyd's List](https://www.lloydslist.com/LL1157327/Hormuz-crisis-side-effect-a-sharp-rise-in-container-shipping-rates) · [Xeneta](https://www.xeneta.com/blog/red-sea-return-what-it-means-for-2026-container-shipping-contract-rates)

- **US-China tariffs remain historically high and are still being actively contested: the effective US tariff rate on Chinese goods sits near 30% (highest of any trading partner), a new 12.5% Section 301 tariff over forced-labor findings was open for comment until 6 July with a hearing 7 July, and the broader US-China tariff truce expires in November 2026 — after which rates could rise significantly if not renewed.** → *For Ari's context:* any China-sourced electronics/hardware (mining components, gallery supplies, general kit) faces both direct tariff exposure (if sourced via US-adjacent supply chains) and indirect cost pass-through from Chinese exporters absorbing tariffs elsewhere; November 2026 is a date worth flagging as a potential cost-shock trigger. [Tax Foundation tariff tracker](https://taxfoundation.org/research/all/federal/trump-tariffs-trade-war/)

- **Australia and China are in open friction over critical minerals, not just trade rhetoric: in May 2026 Australia ordered Chinese investors to divest stakes in Northern Minerals (rare earths), while China's own new export-control regulations on rare earths and processing technology entered force on 15 June 2026, formalizing controls that had been escalating in waves through 2025.** Australia is hedging via a March-2026 EU free-trade deal (tariff-free lithium/rare earths access) and a US$3.5bn US-Australia critical-minerals framework, but lacks the domestic processing capacity to quickly reduce China dependence. → *For Ari's context:* this is a slow-burn structural risk (not an immediate price shock) — it signals AUD and ASX-resources-sector volatility risk is likely to persist through 2026 regardless of the Hormuz outcome, reinforcing the "choppy, headline-driven" market read from yesterday's markets digest rather than a one-off event. [CSIS](https://www.csis.org/analysis/unpacking-us-australia-critical-minerals-framework-agreement) · [Discovery Alert](https://discoveryalert.com.au/china-rare-earth-export-controls-critical-minerals-trade-2026/)

- **Analysts frame July 2026's global economic outlook as being driven more by geopolitical risk and trade-route stability than by traditional growth data — citing Red Sea disruption, Middle East tensions, Panama Canal restrictions, and Taiwan-strait uncertainty as a stacked, simultaneous set of chokepoint risks rather than a single crisis.** → *For Ari's context:* the reasonable planning assumption is a "higher-cost, higher-volatility normal" for the rest of 2026 across imported goods, energy-linked prices, and market sentiment — not a return to pre-2026 baseline once any single crisis (e.g. Hormuz) resolves. [ING Think](https://think.ing.com/articles/lingering-geopolitical-uncertainty-requires-a-crude-rethink/)
